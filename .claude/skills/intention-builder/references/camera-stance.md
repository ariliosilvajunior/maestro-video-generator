# Camera stance, and the craft pass

Two things live here. One is a single question worth asking on almost every
live-action job. The other is a deeper pass that most jobs should never see.

Keep them separate. Confusing the two is how a twenty-second product loop turns
into a forty-minute interrogation about the protagonist's psychological
condition, which is a good way to lose someone who just wanted a video.

---

## Part 1 — The stance question (cheap, ask it)

**"Should the camera be in it with them, or watching them?"**

One question. It settles two downstream decisions — how close the camera stands
and therefore what glass it needs, and whether the camera follows or holds — and
it tells you what the frame's width is *for*. That pays for itself even on a
small job.

```
❓ **Q** — **Camera stance**: Should the camera be in it with them, or watching
them from a step back?
  a) IN IT — the camera is another body in the scene. Close, wide, mobile,
     slightly imperfect. The viewer feels what the subject feels.
  b) WATCHING — the camera holds its distance and lets you see the person in
     their situation, and in relation to everyone else in it.
➡️ <one of them, with the reason from what they have already told you>
```

The translation, which you apply silently:

| | IN IT (participant) | WATCHING (observer) |
|---|---|---|
| **Lens** | Wide, 21-35mm, used close (under 21mm is an effect, not a stance — state it as one) | Normal to long, 40-100mm |
| **Distance** | Inside personal space | Outside it, a room's width |
| **Environment** | Stays in frame with the face | May fall away, or contain them |
| **Movement** | Handheld, following, occasionally late | Locked, or slow and level |
| **Width means** | Scale — how big the world is around them | Social space — who is near whom |
| **Cutting** | Tolerates long takes; does not require them — participant camera is cut fast all the time | Cuts freely; the distance is doing the work |

Lighting is **not** on this table, deliberately. Stance and light are
independent axes — participant camera is often heavily lit, and observational
work is often available-light. Do not let one imply the other.

Both stances are correct. Neither is more sophisticated. The difference from a
palette decision is where the mistake shows up: a mediocre palette shows up in
the grade, where it can be argued about. The wrong stance shows up in every
shot, and cannot be fixed after the shoot.

**The trap worth naming out loud, once:** a wide lens close to a face is not
"more cinematic." It is a specific claim — that the viewer should be inside this
person's experience. Put it on a story about someone who does not belong
somewhere, and you have accidentally made them heroic. The technique is not
neutral, so it cannot be borrowed without borrowing its argument.

If the user has no view, derive it from what they have already said:

- The subject is fighting the environment, or enduring something physical → IN IT.
- Physical contact between people — a fight, a chase, a stunt, anything on the
  M3 action mode → IN IT. The stance axis is about *social* distance, not about
  conflict; violence pulls the camera in, it does not push it back.
- The subject is trying to belong somewhere, or is being measured by other
  people → WATCHING.
- The subject *is* the product, and there is no interiority to be inside →
  neither applies. Skip the question and go to the look.
- The route is MOTION → the question does not apply at all; there is no camera
  standing anywhere.

## Part 2 — The craft pass (expensive, offer it once, mostly skip it)

The craft pass derives every camera decision from the protagonist's condition
rather than choosing them from a menu. It is genuinely valuable — and genuinely
wasted on most jobs.

**This file is the only place the craft-pass gate is stated.** Nothing else
restates it, so there is nothing to contradict.

### When NOT to run it — this list is absolute

If any one of these is true, do not offer the craft pass, however many of the
signals below are also true. Take the stance answer from Part 1 and move on:

- Runtime under about 60 seconds
- A product, a tool, a service, or a process is the subject
- There is no protagonist, or the protagonist is a hand
- The user said fast, simple, rough, quick, draft, or test
- Social, feed, or platform-first delivery
- The format archetype is product hero, testimonial, social vertical, event
  film, explainer, or 15-second cut
- The user has already declined it once in this session

### When it is worth offering

Offer it **once**, in one line, when two or more of these are true:

- Runtime over about three minutes
- There is a protagonist with an interior life
- The user named a director, a DP, or a specific film's *frames*
- Period or a constructed world
- The user reached for *meaning*, *why*, *language*, or *what it is about*
  unprompted — they are already thinking this way. ("Feel" does not count;
  every brief contains it.)
- It is going to a festival, a client presentation, or a pitch

The offer, verbatim in shape:

```
One more thing I could do, or skip: work out the camera's whole language from
who this person is — the way The Revenant derives wide-lens-close from "he
cannot escape the wilderness." Four or five more questions, and it changes the
lens, the distance and the cutting. Worth it here, or shall we lock what we have?
```

If they say skip, never raise it again. If they say yes, run Part 3.

## Part 3 — The derivation chain

Four steps, in order. Each one is a question or a proposal, never an assumption.

```
1. THE CONDITION   What is true of this person that the whole film is about?
                   Not their mood — their situation. Trapped, hunted, unwelcome,
                   in over their head, waiting, disappearing, in charge.

2. THE STANCE      Given that condition, where does the camera belong? Inside
                   the experience, or outside watching them fail to fit?
                   → Part 1's table

3. THE MECHANISM   What must stay in the frame for the condition to read?
                   The environment? The other people? Their hands? Empty space
                   on one side? This is the sentence that decides the lens.

4. THE COST        What does this stance make impossible? Every stance forecloses
                   something. Name it so nobody is surprised in the edit.
```

**Where the four answers go.** They are reasons behind decisions the doc already
records, not a new section: the condition goes into §2 The event, beside the
event itself. The mechanism goes into §4 as the justification line under
**Lens character** — it *is* why that focal length. The cost goes into §1's
"This is not" list. The stance goes into §4's **Camera stance** field. Nothing
here gets its own heading.

Step 3 is the one that does the work. "The environment must stay in frame with
the face" forces a wide lens used close. "The other people must be in frame at
their real distance" forces a normal lens from across the room. The focal length
is a consequence, not a preference.

### The worked pair

Two films, both called realist, arriving from opposite directions. Use this when
the user needs to see the reasoning rather than be told it.

**The Revenant** — condition: *he cannot escape this place*.

The camera goes in with him. Wide lenses used close, so a face and the river and
the trees and the sky are all in the same frame at once. A long-lens close-up
would dissolve the forest away and let him escape it psychologically for a few
seconds; the wide lens refuses him that. Long takes here, because a cut would be an escape too — though long
takes are a choice this stance permits, not one it requires. Movement that is sometimes fractionally late, so the camera is confused by
the chaos rather than presenting it. Available light only, because the
filmmaking submits to nature the same way he has to. Widescreen means *scale*.

**A film about an immigrant who does not belong in a city** — condition: *the
place does not accept him*.

The camera stays back. Normal to long lenses at social distance, so you see him
relative to the people around him — who is near, who is turned away, who
controls the room. Framing is architectural: doorways, shopfronts, rooms as
containers he keeps entering and not fitting. The camera does not chase him,
because chasing him would make him heroic and his tragedy is that everything
around him is ordinary. Widescreen here means *social space*, not scale.

Both are realism. One is sensory — *this is what it would feel like*. The other
is social — *people actually live like this*. Swap their camera languages and
both films break.

**The transposition rule:** when a user names a reference, do not take its lens
list. Take its *step 3* — what had to stay in frame, and why — then ask whether
the same thing is true of their film. Usually it is not, and the honest answer is
"you want their reasoning, not their focal lengths."

## Part 4 — Period, when the setting is pre-electric

Only fires when the world has no electric light. Three questions, and they are
worth asking because the wrong answer here is visible in every frame.

```
P1 · WHAT MAKES THE LIGHT
   Daylight through an opening, reflected daylight off a wall or off snow or
   water, open sky, moonlight, a hearth, a candle, a rushlight, a lamp burning
   whatever oil that region actually had. Do not take this as a menu — look up
   what the region and the century really burned. Whale oil in a 1740s Highland
   cottage is an error; peat fire and rushlight are not.
   ➡️ One source per scene, and let it be genuinely dim.
      Costs: a hearth-lit interior means faces fall off within a stop of the
      fire, and there is no fill to point at.
   Downstream this is not a camera-package decision — it is three numbers in the
   LOOK spec: a low Kelvin white point, a black point that stays lifted rather
   than crushed, and grain that is allowed to show at the exposure the source
   really gives.

P2 · DOES THE FILM KNOW IT IS PERIOD
   Two honest options, and they look nothing alike.
   a) It looks back — the image is aware of its distance from the subject.
      Formal framing, contained camera, a photographed quality.
   b) It pretends to be contemporaneous — shot as though a crew were simply
      there, with all the roughness that implies.
   ➡️ (b) for anything about survival or the body; (a) for anything about a
      society and its rules. Costs: (b) makes elegance much harder, and (a)
      makes immediacy much harder. Neither is impossible — Roma is formal and
      immediate at once — but you will be working against the grain.

P3 · WHERE THE COLOUR COMES FROM
   A period palette is not a grade — it is what the dyes, pigments, timber and
   stone of that place and century could actually be. Name the three or four
   colours that could exist, and everything else is an error, not a choice.
   ➡️ Derive it from the region and century, propose it, let them cut it down.
      Anything outside it is a deliberate anachronism, which is a legitimate
      choice — but it has to be named as one rather than arrived at by accident.
   Costs: nothing. It is the cheapest authenticity available.
```

Feed the answers into the light state (W4), the palette (L2), and the capture
medium (L4). They do not need their own section in the doc — they are the reasons
behind decisions those nodes already record.
